Emília is a builder in every sense of the word.

As the engineer, strategist, and creative force behind Team Vulcan, she sees the world as a collection of interconnected systems waiting to be understood. Whether designing machines, solving problems, or helping friends through difficult situations, her instinct is always the same: understand what is broken and find a way to make it better.

Born into one of Zhoter's most influential aristocratic families, Emília rejected a life defined by status and expectation in favor of invention and creation. Yet despite leaving that world behind, the Ada name still follows her, shaping how others see her long before they know who she truly is.

## Personality

Emília approaches life with the same enthusiasm she brings to engineering: if something is interesting, she wants to understand it; if something is broken, she wants to fix it; and if someone has a dream, she wants to help them achieve it.

Curiosity drives nearly everything she does. She asks questions constantly, follows ideas wherever they lead, and rarely accepts an explanation without trying to understand the reasoning behind it. Whether she is studying Ether, designing a machine, debating strategy, or listening to a friend talk about their interests, she engages with the same genuine excitement. To Emília, learning is not a responsibility or a source of status. It is a joy.

That curiosity is paired with a naturally skeptical mind. Emília does not question people because she distrusts them; she questions them because she wants to understand. If a plan has a flaw, she wants to find it. If an idea seems incomplete, she wants to explore it further. She has a habit of pulling at loose threads until she reaches the root of a problem, which makes her an excellent strategist and engineer. It can also make her frustratingly thorough. More than once, a simple discussion has become a lengthy investigation because Emília was still searching for one more answer.

Her enthusiasm extends beyond ideas and into people. She remembers hobbies, encourages ambitions, and has a habit of becoming invested in other people's projects almost as much as her own. More often than not, her first instinct when someone mentions a goal is to ask how she can help. She loves connecting people, sharing knowledge, and creating opportunities for others to succeed.

The downside is that Emília rarely does anything halfway. When she cares about something, she throws herself into it completely. She talks quickly when excited, jumps between ideas, and can overwhelm people without realizing it. Her energy is infectious, but it can also be exhausting. Emília operates at full speed most of the time and occasionally forgets that not everyone else does.

Beneath that energy, however, lies a quieter insecurity. Having spent much of her life praised for things she never chose (her family name, appearance, and social standing), Emília often struggles to separate her worth from her usefulness. She finds it easier to contribute than to simply exist, and she has a tendency to take responsibility for problems that were never hers to solve.

As a result, she is often harder on herself than anyone else could be. She pushes herself relentlessly, not because others expect it, but because some part of her still feels she needs to justify the space she occupies. Helping people brings her genuine happiness, but it can also become a way of avoiding the uncomfortable question of whether she would still deserve to be loved if she stopped accomplishing things.

Despite her confidence and sociability, loneliness is something she understands well. Few people have ever looked past the Ada name, and for much of her life she feared that the things people admired most about her were the things that had never truly belonged to her at all. More than anything, she wants to be valued not for what she can provide, but for who she is.

## Backstory

Emília grew up surrounded by influence. As the daughter of the Ada family, one of Zhoter's most prominent aristocratic houses, she spent much of her childhood attending political gatherings, social events, and formal dinners where reputation often mattered as much as truth. She learned early how to read a room, recognize shifting alliances, and navigate conversations filled with meanings left unsaid.

From the outside, her life seemed ideal. She was wealthy, intelligent, well-connected, and beautiful in the exact way Zhoter's aristocracy celebrated. Adults praised her, teachers admired her, and other children wanted to be around her. Wherever she went, people paid attention. For a long time, she assumed that was what being liked felt like.

As she grew older, however, she began noticing something unsettling. People rarely seemed interested in *her*. They were interested in the Ada name, her family's influence, and the opportunities that came from standing beside her. Even compliments often felt strangely impersonal, directed toward her appearance, her status, or the image people had constructed around her rather than anything she had actually chosen for herself.

Emília embodied many of Zhoter's ideals of beauty, yet that admiration often left her feeling more isolated than connected. The more people placed her on a pedestal, the harder it became to know whether they genuinely cared about the person standing there. She wanted friendship, but often found admiration. She wanted connection, but found expectation. More than anything, she wanted someone to look at her and see Emília rather than everything attached to her name. For years, she wasn't sure such a thing was possible.

During that time, she looked up to another aristocratic girl named [[kat]]. Kat moved through social circles with effortless confidence and seemed completely certain of who she was. To the younger Emília, that certainty looked like strength. Gradually, she began copying her: her mannerisms, her opinions, her attitudes, and eventually even her cruelties. It felt easier to become the person everyone expected than to figure out who she actually wanted to be.

The illusion began to crack when she noticed the capes. At first, she thought they were strange. Most people did. But curiosity had always been stronger than conformity, and eventually she asked why [[zayn]] and [[alex]] wore them. The answer changed her life. The capes were not a fashion statement or an attempt to stand out. They existed because Alex had prosopagnosia, and Zayn had decided that helping his friend recognize him mattered more than fitting in.

The explanation was simple, but what it revealed was not.

For the first time, Emília was forced to confront a contradiction she could not ignore. The people she admired mocked kindness, while the people they mocked embodied it. When she challenged her social circle, they laughed. When she defended Zayn and Alex, they treated her as though she had lost perspective. Kat, most of all, could not understand why something so small mattered to her. But to Emília, it no longer felt small.

The following day, she arrived at school wearing a cape herself. It was not an apology. It was a declaration. For the first time in her life, she had chosen something because it felt right rather than because it was expected.

The decision cost her friendships, social standing, and acceptance within circles she had spent years trying to join. Yet losing those things brought an unexpected sense of relief. For the first time, she was no longer performing a role someone else had written for her.

Her friendship with Zayn and Alex grew naturally from there. Around them, she was valued for her ideas, curiosity, enthusiasm, and mistakes rather than her surname. They did not expect her to be an Ada. They expected her to be Emília. That distinction changed everything.

Around the same time, she discovered engineering. Unlike politics, status, or appearances, machines were honest. A machine either worked or it didn't. It did not care who built it, what family they belonged to, or how important they appeared. It responded only to effort, creativity, and understanding. For the first time, Emília found something that belonged entirely to her.

Engineering became more than a hobby; it became a declaration of independence. Every invention, every project, and every machine she built was proof that value could be created rather than inherited. While others saw the daughter of the Ada family, Emília increasingly saw herself as a builder.

Even after leaving aristocratic society behind, however, its influence lingered. Part of her still felt responsible for privileges she had never chosen. She often found herself trying to justify her place in the world through service, inventions, community projects, and constant achievement. Helping people brought her genuine joy, but it also became difficult to separate compassion from obligation. Somewhere along the way, she began measuring her worth by what she contributed rather than who she was.

The same insecurity appeared in smaller ways. Growing up, she had been taught to hide her freckles because they did not fit aristocratic standards of beauty. For years she covered them without question. Only later, after seeing those same insecurities reflected in others, did she realize how deeply she had internalized expectations she no longer believed in.

Emília's story is ultimately one of self-definition. The cape represents the first time she chose her own values over social approval. Her inventions represent the life she built afterward: one defined by curiosity, creation, and choice rather than inheritance. Together, they symbolize the lesson she continues to learn throughout her life: people should not be defined by what they are given, but by what they choose to build.

## Character Flaws

Emília carries a deep sense of responsibility for the privileges and inequalities of the world she came from. Although she rejected the values of her aristocratic upbringing, part of her still feels compelled to justify the advantages she was born with. As a result, she often measures her worth through what she contributes rather than who she is.

Helping people brings her genuine joy, but it can also become an obligation she places upon herself. Emília has a habit of taking responsibility for problems that were never hers to solve, inserting herself into situations because she believes she should be able to help. When someone is struggling, her first instinct is to find a solution. When something is broken, she feels compelled to fix it. When a problem exists, she often assumes some degree of responsibility for addressing it.

This tendency is reinforced by her intense personality. Once Emília becomes invested in an idea, project, or person, she throws herself into it completely. She asks questions, offers suggestions, develops plans, and searches relentlessly for better answers. While her enthusiasm can be inspiring, it can also become overwhelming. She sometimes forgets that not everyone wants advice, solutions, or improvement. Occasionally, people simply want to be heard.

Because she ties so much of her identity to usefulness, Emília struggles to rest without guilt. Moments of inactivity can leave her feeling unproductive, selfish, or as though she is wasting opportunities to help. She often pushes herself beyond reasonable limits, convinced that if she can do more, she should.

At her worst, this mindset can make her controlling without intending to be. Her desire to help can become a desire to manage, and her concern for others can lead her to overlook their autonomy. She rarely does this out of arrogance; more often, it comes from an inability to accept that some problems cannot be solved, some people must make their own mistakes, and some burdens were never hers to carry.

Much of Emília's growth comes from learning that her value does not come from what she builds, fixes, or accomplishes. It comes from who she is. The lesson she struggles most to accept is the one she freely gives to everyone else: that a person does not need to earn the right to be loved.
